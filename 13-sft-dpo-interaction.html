An Empirical Study of SFT-DPO Interaction and Parameterization in Small Language Models

Feng & Yang
arXiv:2603.20100
March 2026
arXiv

This paper systematically dissects the interaction between Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) in small language models, using GPT-2 (124M parameters) as a controlled experimental platform. The central finding is striking: parameterization choice (full fine-tuning vs. LoRA) dominates the performance gap more than the objective function choice (SFT vs. DPO). The paper quantifies this as a 2.17-point gap attributable to parameterization versus a 0.18-point gap attributable to the training objective, fundamentally challenging the assumption that adding DPO always improves alignment quality.

1. Research Overview and Motivation

The standard alignment pipeline in modern LLM development follows a two-stage recipe: first, Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on instruction-following demonstrations, then Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) on human preference pairs to further refine the model's behavior. This SFT → DPO pipeline has become so ubiquitous that many practitioners apply it without questioning whether DPO actually adds value, or whether the gains are better attributed to other design choices.

Feng & Yang set out to answer a fundamental question: when you observe a performance difference between two aligned models, how much of that difference comes from the training objective (SFT vs. DPO) versus the parameterization (full fine-tuning vs. low-rank adapters)? This is not merely an academic curiosity—it directly impacts how researchers allocate compute budgets and engineering effort in alignment pipelines.

🔬
Core Research Questions: (1) Does adding DPO after strong SFT consistently improve performance? (2) How does parameterization (FFT vs. LoRA) interact with the SFT-DPO pipeline? (3) At what SFT quality level does DPO provide meaningful marginal gains? (4) How does SFT data scale affect the DPO benefit?

The experimental design is deliberately constrained: a single base model (GPT-2, 124M parameters), two task domains (paraphrase detection and sonnet generation), and a systematic grid over parameterization, training stages, and data scales. This control enables clean causal attribution that larger-scale studies cannot achieve due to confounds.

2. Experimental Design and Setup

2.1 Base Model and Tasks

The study uses GPT-2 (124M parameters) as the base model for all experiments. While GPT-2 is small by current standards, this is a deliberate choice: its limited capacity makes it more sensitive to training decisions, amplifying effects that might be masked in larger models. Two fundamentally different tasks are selected to test generalizability:

Paraphrase Detection — a classification task using the Quora Question Pairs dataset with 283,000 training examples. The model must determine whether two questions are semantically equivalent. This tests natural language understanding and the ability to learn decision boundaries.

Sonnet Generation — a generative task where the model produces Shakespearean sonnets conditioned on a theme. This tests creative generation, adherence to structural constraints (iambic pentameter, ABAB CDCD EFEF GG rhyme scheme), and stylistic control. The evaluation uses multiple DPO preference strategies (V1, V2, V3) with varying temperature settings.

2.2 Parameterization Conditions

The paper defines three parameterization regimes, each representing a fundamentally different optimization landscape:

Parameterization Description Trainable Parameters Key Properties
FFT (Full Fine-Tuning) All 124M parameters updated 124M (100%) Maximum expressiveness, highest risk of catastrophic forgetting
LoRA r=4 Rank-4 low-rank adapters ~0.5M (~0.4%) Most constrained, lowest capacity for new behaviors
LoRA r=8 Rank-8 low-rank adapters ~1M (~0.8%) Moderate capacity, balanced constraint
LoRA r=16 Rank-16 low-rank adapters ~2M (~1.6%) Highest LoRA capacity tested, closest to FFT expressiveness

For reference on LoRA mechanics, see Paper 6: LoRA. The key insight here is that LoRA rank determines the hypothesis space: rank 4 provides only 4 orthogonal directions per layer for learning new behaviors, which may be insufficient for complex alignment tasks. This connects directly to the parameterization dominance finding.

2.3 Training Stage Configurations

The paper tests five distinct training configurations that systematically vary when (and whether) DPO is applied:

1

SFT-Only

Supervised fine-tuning on instruction data for N epochs. No preference optimization. This is the baseline.

2

DPO-Only

Direct preference optimization applied directly to the base model without any SFT warm-start. Tests whether DPO can replace SFT.

3

SFT → DPO (Epoch 3)

SFT for 3 epochs, then switch to DPO. Early intervention point—DPO applied while the model is still learning from demonstrations.

4

SFT → DPO (Epoch 9)

SFT for 9 epochs, then DPO. Late intervention—DPO applied after the model has substantially converged on demonstrations.

5

Staged SFT → DPO

Full SFT convergence followed by DPO with careful hyperparameter tuning. Represents the "best practice" pipeline.

∞

DPO Hyperparameters

Learning rate: 5×10⁻⁶. Beta (KL penalty): 0.2. These are kept fixed across all conditions for controlled comparison.

3. Paraphrase Detection Results

The paraphrase detection task reveals the most striking finding of the paper: parameterization choice dominates the objective function choice. Here are the detailed results across all conditions:

Configuration Accuracy (%) F1 Score (%) Δ from FFT Baseline
FFT SFT-Only 89.87 89.21 — (baseline)
FFT SFT → DPO 90.05 89.39 +0.18 F1
LoRA r=16 SFT-Only 88.42 87.78 −1.43 F1
LoRA r=16 SFT → DPO 88.60 87.96 −1.25 F1
LoRA r=8 SFT-Only 87.95 87.32 −1.89 F1
LoRA r=4 SFT-Only 87.70 87.00 −2.21 F1

"The parameterization gap between FFT and LoRA (2.17 points) is more than 12 times larger than the objective gap between SFT-only and SFT→DPO (0.18 points). Choosing full fine-tuning over LoRA matters far more than adding DPO after SFT."

The key takeaways from paraphrase detection are:

FFT vs. LoRA gap is massive. Full fine-tuning achieves 89.21 F1 while the best LoRA configuration (r=16) reaches only 87.78 F1—a 1.43-point gap that no amount of DPO tuning can close. This suggests that for classification tasks on relatively small models, the ability to update all parameters is critical for learning complex decision boundaries. This is consistent with findings that LoRA's low-rank constraint limits the hypothesis space for tasks requiring broad representational changes.

DPO provides marginal gains regardless of parameterization. Across all configurations, DPO adds approximately 0.18 F1 points. This gain is consistent but small—it represents a ~2% relative improvement over the SFT baseline. The gain does not vary significantly between FFT and LoRA regimes, suggesting DPO's marginal benefit is orthogonal to the parameterization effect.

LoRA rank scaling shows diminishing returns. Moving from r=4 to r=8 provides a 0.32 F1 improvement, while moving from r=8 to r=16 provides only 0.46 F1 improvement. Extrapolating, even very high LoRA ranks would not close the gap with FFT, supporting the view that full parameter updates enable qualitatively different learning dynamics.

3.1 Data Scale Effects

The paper conducts a critical ablation on SFT data scale, testing performance at full scale (283K examples), 10% scale (28.3K), and 1% scale (2.83K):

SFT Data Scale F1 Score (%) Δ from Full Scale
283K (full) 88.2 — (baseline)
28.3K (10%) 83.5 −4.7 F1
2.83K (1%) 77.2 −11.0 F1

The data scale finding is dramatic: reducing SFT data from 283K to 2.83K (a 100× reduction) drops F1 by 11 points—five times larger than the entire DPO benefit. This has profound practical implications: the effort spent curating a large, high-quality SFT dataset is far more valuable than the effort spent on DPO preference data. Moreover, DPO cannot compensate for insufficient SFT data, because DPO assumes a reasonable base policy to optimize from.

📊
Data Scale Hierarchy: Full SFT data (283K) gives 88.2 F1. At 10% scale (28.3K), performance drops to 83.5 F1. At 1% scale (2.83K), it falls to 77.2 F1. The 11-point drop at 1% scale dwarfs all other experimental variations combined. This establishes that SFT data quantity is the single most important hyperparameter in the alignment pipeline.

4. Sonnet Generation Results

The sonnet generation task provides a complementary perspective: unlike classification, this is an open-ended generative task where evaluation is more nuanced. The paper introduces three DPO variant strategies (V1, V2, V3) that differ in how preference pairs are constructed, and evaluates across a range of sampling temperatures.

4.1 Temperature Sensitivity

A key finding in the sonnet generation experiments is the temperature-dependent performance peak. The best results occur at T=1.5, which is higher than the typical default of T=0.7–1.0 used in most deployment scenarios. This suggests that for creative generation tasks, the DPO-optimized policy benefits from higher exploration during sampling, potentially because DPO creates a more peaked reward landscape that requires temperature to navigate effectively.

The three DPO strategies (V1, V2, V3) show different sensitivity to temperature, with V1 (preference based on structural adherence) being most sensitive and V3 (preference based on thematic coherence) being most robust across temperatures. This indicates that the type of preference signal interacts with sampling parameters in non-trivial ways.

4.2 DPO Strategy Comparison

DPO Strategy Preference Basis Best Temperature Sensitivity to Temp
V1 (Structural) Rhyme scheme, meter adherence T=1.5 High
V2 (Hybrid) Structural + semantic quality T=1.2–1.5 Medium
V3 (Thematic) Thematic coherence, creativity T=1.0–1.5 Low

The sonnet results reinforce the paper's central thesis: DPO provides marginal improvements over strong SFT, and these improvements are sensitive to both the preference signal design and the sampling parameters. The interaction between DPO strategy and temperature demonstrates that DPO does not create a universally better policy—it creates a policy that is better along specific dimensions, and extracting that improvement requires careful inference-time tuning.

5. The Parameterization > Objective Finding

The paper's most significant contribution is the rigorous quantification of the relative importance of parameterization versus training objective. The metric is straightforward: measure the performance gap attributable to each factor while holding the other constant.

"When we decompose the total performance variance into parameterization effects and objective effects, the ratio is approximately 12:1. The community's focus on optimizing the training objective (SFT vs. DPO vs. other approaches) may be misguided when the parameterization choice is left unexamined."

5.1 Decomposition Analysis

The decomposition works as follows. For any two model configurations A and B, the total performance gap Δ(A,B) can be decomposed as: 

Δ(FFT-SFT, LoRA-DPO) = Δ(FFT→LoRA) + Δ(SFT→DPO) + Δ(interaction)
                       = 2.17           + 0.18          + 0.05 (estimated)
                       ≈ 2.40 total gap

The parameterization gap (2.17) represents the performance difference when switching from FFT to LoRA while holding the objective constant. The objective gap (0.18) represents the performance difference when adding DPO while holding parameterization constant. The interaction term (0.05) captures any non-additive effects.

5.2 Why Parameterization Dominates

The paper offers several explanations for why parameterization matters so much more than the training objective in this setting:

Hypothesis space capacity. Full fine-tuning can modify all 124M parameters, enabling the model to learn fundamentally new representations. LoRA with r=4 modifies only ~0.5M parameters through low-rank updates, severely constraining what the model can learn. For the paraphrase detection task, which requires learning complex semantic comparison patterns, the low-rank constraint prevents the model from fully internalizing the task structure.

Gradient flow dynamics. FFT allows gradients to flow through all parameters, enabling coordinated updates across the network. LoRA updates are applied additively to frozen weights, creating a mismatch between the gradient signal (which flows through the adapter) and the representation (which is fixed in the base weights). This is particularly problematic for DPO, which requires precise preference-weighted gradients.

Initialization sensitivity. LoRA adapters are typically initialized with small random values (often using Gaussian initialization scaled by 1/√r), meaning the initial policy is close to the base model. DPO's effectiveness depends on the initial policy being sufficiently different from the reference policy to enable meaningful gradient signals. If the LoRA-initialized policy is too close to the base, DPO gradients become vanishingly small.

This connects to findings in Paper 6: LoRA, where the rank and initialization determine the effective learning capacity, and to Paper 10: DPO, where the implicit reward structure depends on the policy being sufficiently expressive.

6. When DPO Helps and When It Doesn't

Beyond the parameterization finding, the paper provides nuanced analysis of the conditions under which DPO adds meaningful value:

When DPO Helps

Strong SFT with aligned preferences: When the SFT data teaches the model a behavior that is directionally aligned with the DPO preference signal, DPO provides incremental refinement. Example: SFT teaches the model to write sonnets, and DPO teaches it which sonnets are better—the preference signal refines an already-learned skill.

FFT parameterization: DPO gains are most consistent and largest when the model uses full fine-tuning, because the full parameter space allows DPO's preference-weighted gradients to make meaningful updates.

Early SFT stage (epoch 3): When DPO is applied early in training—before the model has fully converged on the SFT objective—it can redirect the learning trajectory toward preferred behaviors more effectively.

When DPO Doesn't Help

Misaligned preference signals: When the DPO preference dataset captures behavioral dimensions that are orthogonal to the SFT objectives, DPO provides no measurable improvement. Example: SFT teaches factual accuracy, but DPO preferences are about style—the model cannot optimize both simultaneously through the DPO gradient signal.

LoRA parameterization: The low-rank constraint limits DPO's ability to make the necessary representational changes, resulting in marginal or negligible gains regardless of preference quality.

Already-converged SFT: When the SFT model has fully converged (epoch 9+), DPO provides the smallest marginal gains because there is less "room" for the preference signal to redirect the learning trajectory.

DPO-only without SFT: Training DPO directly on the base model without SFT warm-start produces poor results because DPO assumes a reasonable initial policy to optimize from. Without SFT, the base model's behavior is too far from the desired distribution for preference gradients to be informative.

The SFT Quality Ceiling Effect

The paper identifies a "ceiling effect" where DPO's marginal benefit decreases as SFT quality increases. This is not because DPO becomes harmful—it consistently adds ~0.18 F1 points—but because the absolute improvement becomes less meaningful relative to the SFT baseline. In practical terms, if SFT achieves 89% accuracy, a 0.18% improvement from DPO represents a ~2% relative gain, which may not justify the additional training complexity, data curation, and compute cost.

This ceiling effect has implications for resource-constrained settings: teams with limited compute budgets may achieve better results by investing all resources in SFT quality (more data, better curation, longer training) rather than adding a DPO stage.

7. DPO Hyperparameter Sensitivity

The paper fixes DPO hyperparameters across all conditions: learning rate = 5×10⁻⁶, beta = 0.2, batch size and other parameters held constant. While this enables clean controlled comparison, the fixed hyperparameters also reveal important properties of DPO's behavior:

Beta = 0.2 (KL penalty coefficient). This is a relatively aggressive KL constraint, meaning DPO is strongly discouraged from deviating from the reference policy. This choice likely contributes to the marginal gains: a lower beta would allow more aggressive preference optimization but risks distributional collapse. The ZEPHYR recipe (see Paper 11) uses beta = 0.1, allowing more flexibility.

Learning rate = 5×10⁻⁶. This is a conservative learning rate for DPO, ensuring stable but slow optimization. Higher learning rates could potentially extract more value from the preference signal, but at the risk of training instability. The paper's choice reflects a preference for controlled, reproducible results over aggressive optimization.

Loss type. The paper uses the standard sigmoid DPO loss. Alternative formulations (hinge, IPO) may behave differently under the same conditions, but this comparison is left to future work. See Paper 10 for DPO loss variants and Paper 12 for ORPO as a reference-model-free alternative.

9. Limitations and Caveats

⚠️
Scope limitations: The findings are specific to GPT-2 (124M parameters) and may not generalize to larger models. LoRA's relative performance may improve with larger base models where the parameter count provides more inherent capacity. The two tasks (paraphrase detection, sonnet generation) may not represent the full spectrum of alignment challenges.

Model scale. The study uses a 124M-parameter model. Larger models (7B, 13B, 70B) may exhibit different dynamics: LoRA's relative performance could improve as the base model provides more inherent representational capacity, potentially narrowing the parameterization gap. Conversely, DPO's marginal benefit might increase with scale as the preference signal becomes more informative relative to the SFT signal.

Limited task diversity. Paraphrase detection (classification) and sonnet generation (creative) represent two task types, but many alignment-relevant tasks fall into other categories: instruction following, factuality, safety refusal, code generation, multi-turn dialogue. The parameterization dominance finding may vary across these task types.

Fixed DPO hyperparameters. While the controlled setting is a strength for attribution, it means the paper does not explore whether DPO's marginal gains could be amplified through hyperparameter optimization. The beta=0.2 and lr=5e-6 choices may be suboptimal for some conditions.

Base model age. GPT-2 is an older architecture without modern improvements (RoPE, SwiGLU, GQA). The findings may differ for models with more modern positional encodings or activation functions. See Paper 16: Phi-3 and Paper 17: Qwen3 for modern architecture choices.

10. Practical Implications for Alignment Pipelines

The paper's findings have direct, actionable implications for practitioners building alignment pipelines:

10.1 Investment Priority

Priority 1: SFT data quality and scale. The 11-point F1 drop from data scale reduction is the largest effect in the study. Invest heavily in collecting, curating, and verifying SFT demonstration data. This connects to findings in Paper 8: LIMA on data quality and Paper 9: Self-Instruct on data generation.

Priority 2: Parameterization choice. If compute allows, use full fine-tuning for the alignment stage. If using LoRA, use the highest rank your VRAM budget permits (at least r=16 for small models). See Paper 7: QLoRA for memory-efficient LoRA training.

Priority 3: DPO (if applicable). Only add DPO after ensuring SFT quality is strong and the preference data is aligned with SFT objectives. Do not assume DPO always helps—measure it empirically on your specific task.

10.2 Diagnostic Framework

The paper implicitly provides a diagnostic framework for deciding whether to add DPO:

Step 1: Evaluate SFT-only performance. If SFT performance is strong (close to your quality target), DPO is likely to provide marginal gains only.

Step 2: Analyze the preference data. Does it capture behaviors that are complementary to (not redundant with) the SFT data? If the preference data is about a dimension that SFT already teaches well, DPO will not help.

Step 3: If both conditions suggest DPO may help, test it with a single epoch of DPO on a held-out set before committing to full DPO training. The marginal gain (0.18 F1 points in this study) should be weighed against the additional complexity.

10.3 Connection to Broader Literature

This paper's findings connect to and inform several other papers in the alignment literature:

  • Paper 6: LoRA — The parameterization dominance finding challenges the assumption that LoRA is always sufficient for alignment tasks
  • Paper 10: DPO — The marginal DPO gains suggest the original DPO paper's results may reflect parameterization choices rather than objective superiority
  • Paper 11: ZEPHYR — The ZEPHYR recipe's beta=0.1 may be more aggressive than the beta=0.2 used here, potentially extracting more value from DPO
  • Paper 12: ORPO — ORPO's single-stage approach avoids the SFT-DPO interaction entirely, potentially sidestepping the issues identified in this paper
  • Paper 14: DeepSeekMath — The GRPO algorithm offers an alternative to DPO that may interact differently with parameterization
