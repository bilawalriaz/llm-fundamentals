LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models

Hu et al. (Microsoft)
arXiv:2106.09685
June 2021
Paper

LoRA proposes that the weight updates ΔW in large language models during fine-tuning have a low intrinsic rank. By reparameterizing ΔW = BA where B ∈ ℝd×r and A ∈ ℝr×k with r ≪ min(d,k), LoRA freezes all pre-trained weights and trains only these low-rank matrices. This reduces trainable parameters by 10,000× (e.g., 35MB vs 350GB for GPT-3) and GPU memory by 3×, while matching or exceeding full fine-tuning performance. After training, the low-rank update is merged into the base weights, producing zero additional inference latency.

The Problem: Why Full Fine-Tuning Is Broken

When a language model with hundreds of billions of parameters needs to adapt to a new task — summarization, dialogue, code generation — the naive approach is to update every parameter in the network. For GPT-3 175B, this means storing and computing gradients for 350GB of weights across multiple GPUs. Full fine-tuning is prohibitively expensive not just in computation, but in storage: each downstream task requires a complete copy of the model, making multi-task deployment impractical.

Prior parameter-efficient approaches each have critical limitations that LoRA was designed to address:

1

Adapter Layers (Houlsby et al., 2019)

Insert small bottleneck layers after each attention block. Adds ~3.6% parameters per adapter but introduces sequential computation, increasing inference latency by 15-30%. Not suitable for latency-sensitive deployment.

2

Prefix Tuning (Li & Liang, 2021)

Prepends trainable vectors to keys and values. Reduces effective sequence length, which degrades performance on long-context tasks. Also suffers from training instability at scale.

3

LoRA (This Paper)

Injects low-rank matrices directly into weight layers. No added latency, no sequence length reduction, no additional inference parameters. The weight update is merged into the base model.

LoRA's key insight is that the adaptation process can be factored into a low-rank decomposition, and this factorization can be applied directly to the weight matrices that already exist in the model, rather than adding new computational layers.

The Low-Rank Adaptation Method

Core Formulation

For a pre-trained weight matrix W0 ∈ ℝd×k, LoRA constrains the update as follows: 

h = W₀ · x + ΔW · x

where:
  ΔW = B · A
  B ∈ ℝ^(d×r)    ← learned
  A ∈ ℝ^(r×k)    ← learned
  rank r ≪ min(d, k)

Total trainable parameters per layer: r(d + k)
vs. full fine-tuning: d × k
Reduction ratio: dk / r(d+k) ≈ k/r for d ≈ k

For a typical transformer with d = k = 4096 and rank r = 4, the reduction is approximately 1024×. Across 32 layers with Wq and Wv adapters, the total trainable parameters are only 2 × 32 × 4 × (4096 + 4096) = ~1M parameters, versus 2 × 32 × 4096² ≈ 1B for full fine-tuning of those same matrices.

Initialization Strategy

The initialization of A and B is deliberately asymmetric and critical to LoRA's behavior: 

A ~ N(0, σ²)    ← random Gaussian initialization
B = 0            ← zero initialization

Result: ΔW = B · A = 0 at initialization

This means the model starts training from exactly the pre-trained weights, with no perturbation. The random initialization of A ensures that the initial gradient signals are well-conditioned — each element of B receives gradients from a different random linear combination of input features. The zero initialization of B ensures that the LoRA module has no effect until training begins, avoiding any cold-start instability.

Scaling Factor α/r

LoRA introduces a scaling factor that controls the magnitude of the low-rank update relative to the pre-trained weights: 

h = W₀ · x + (α/r) · B · A · x

When α = r: the update is unscaled
When α = 1: the update is scaled by 1/r
When α = 2r: the update is amplified 2×

The scaling factor α/r is a hyperparameter that interacts with the learning rate. In practice, setting α = r and tuning the learning rate is equivalent to fixing α and adjusting the learning rate. The authors found that fixing α and varying r gives more intuitive control: as r increases, the model capacity grows, but the per-parameter contribution shrinks proportionally, leading to the observed plateau in performance.

Which Weights to Adapt

LoRA can theoretically be applied to any weight matrix in a transformer — Wq, Wk, Wv, Wo (output projection), and the MLP layers Wup, Wdown, Wgate. The paper systematically evaluates which combinations work best under a fixed parameter budget:

Key Finding: Wq + Wv is the best combination for a given parameter budget. Spreading the budget across more matrices (including Wk and Wo) does not improve performance — it actually slightly degrades it. This suggests that the most important adaptation happens in the query and value projections, which control what information the model attends to and what information it retrieves.

Note that this finding from the original LoRA paper was later superseded by QLoRA (see [Paper 07]), which found that applying LoRA to all transformer layers — including MLP layers — is critical when combining LoRA with quantization. The attention-only recommendation applies specifically to the non-quantized, high-precision setting of the original paper.

Evidence for Intrinsic Low-Rank Structure

One of the most important contributions of the LoRA paper is empirical evidence that weight updates during fine-tuning have low intrinsic rank. The paper's case rests on how rank affects accuracy (Table 6), how the update's dominant directions behave across ranks and random seeds (Figures 3, 4, 6, 7), and how ΔW relates to the pre-trained weights (Section 7.3, Appendix H).

Analysis 1: Subspace Similarity Across Ranks and Seeds

The authors compare the top singular directions of ΔWq and ΔWv from a rank-8 adapter with those from a rank-64 adapter, and separately compare two runs with different random seeds. In both cases the dominant directions overlap substantially: the normalised subspace similarity for the top directions is close to 1, and it decays for the lower directions. The paper reports this as Figures 3 and 4, and generalises it to other layers in Figures 6 and 7. The leading directions of the update are stable across rank and seed, which is what makes a low rank sufficient.

ΔW Amplification vs. Duplication

A critical question is whether the low-rank update ΔW merely duplicates what's already in the pre-trained weights W0, or whether it amplifies task-specific features. The authors analyse this on GPT-3 (layer 48) by comparing the subspaces spanned by W0 and ΔW: 

Spectral analysis (GPT-3, layer 48, q_proj):

‖W_q‖_F  = 6.91
‖ΔW_q‖_F = 3.57
Amplification factor (r=4):
  ‖ΔW‖_F / ‖UᵀWVᵀ‖_F ≈ 21.5

Subspace similarity between ΔW's top singular
directions and the top 10% of W's: below 0.2.

The paper draws three conclusions. ΔW correlates more with W than a random matrix does, so it amplifies features already present in W. But it does not repeat W's top singular directions; it amplifies directions that are important yet under-emphasised during pre-training. For r=4 the amplification factor is about 21.5, falling to about 2 at r=64. That is the "amplification" phenomenon the authors identify: LoRA re-weights task-specific directions rather than learning them from scratch.

The pre-trained model already holds a rich representation. Adaptation does not need to learn new knowledge; it needs to amplify task-specific features that were under-emphasised during pre-training. LoRA does this with remarkable parameter efficiency.

Rank Analysis and Hyperparameter Sensitivity

How Much Rank Do You Need?

One of the most practically useful findings in the paper is the relationship between rank r and downstream performance:

Key: rank 1 is enough to adapt W_q and W_v together
on both datasets; adapting W_q alone needs more rank.

Rank barely moves accuracy once the right weight matrices are adapted. The paper's reading is that the update lives in a low-dimensional subspace, and which subspace you adapt matters more than how large the rank is.

Learning Rate and Batch Size

The paper recommends the following hyperparameter settings, which were found to be robust across tasks:

AdamW optimizer Learning rate: 1e-4 to 3e-4 Batch size: 128 Epochs: 2 Warmup: linear (first 6% of steps) Weight decay: 0.1

LoRA is remarkably insensitive to these hyperparameters. The learning rate can vary by an order of magnitude (1e-4 to 3e-4) without significant performance degradation. This is in stark contrast to full fine-tuning, where learning rate is one of the most critical hyperparameters. The authors attribute this stability to the constrained parameter space — with only r(d + k) parameters per layer, the optimization landscape is smoother and more forgiving.

Inference: Zero Latency, Weight Merging

LoRA's most important deployment advantage is that the low-rank matrices can be merged into the base weights at no additional cost: 

Training time:
  h = W₀ · x + (α/r) · B · A · x

Deployment (merge once):
  W_new = W₀ + (α/r) · B · A

Inference:
  h = W_new · x    ← identical to original model
  No extra FLOPs, no extra memory, no extra latency

This is fundamentally different from adapter-based methods, which add sequential computation at inference time. With LoRA, the adapter is "absorbed" into the base weights, producing a model that is architecturally identical to the original. This means:

  • No inference overhead: The merged model runs at exactly the same speed as the original pre-trained model.
  • No architecture changes: The model's forward pass, attention patterns, and computational graph are unchanged.
  • No additional memory: The base model and LoRA weights can be combined and the LoRA weights discarded.

Task Switching

For serving multiple tasks from a single base model, LoRA enables extremely efficient task switching:

🔄
Task Switching Protocol: Keep one copy of W0 in memory. Store small LoRA modules (0.3-4.7MB each) for each task. To switch tasks, simply compute W0 + BA for the target task and serve. For 100 tasks, this requires ~350GB base model + 350MB total LoRA modules — vs. 100 × 350GB = 35TB for full fine-tuning.

Practical Implementation Guide

Choosing Rank r

r = 4-8 is sufficient for most tasks. Use r = 4 as a starting point and increase only if performance is critical and the task is complex. For most practical applications, r = 4 gives > 99% of the performance of r = 64. Higher ranks are only necessary when the fine-tuning task diverges significantly from the pre-training distribution (e.g., adapting an English model to a non-English language).

Choosing Target Matrices

Wq + Wv is the default recommendation for non-quantized models. This combination captures both "what to attend to" (query) and "what to retrieve" (value). For quantized models (QLoRA), apply LoRA to all linear layers including MLP — this is the critical finding from [Paper 07]. When in doubt, start with Wq + Wv and expand only if needed.

Learning Rate and Training

Learning rate: 2e-4 with AdamW optimizer. Batch size 128, 2 epochs with linear warmup for 6% of steps. LoRA is surprisingly insensitive to learning rate — a range of [1e-4, 3e-4] works across most tasks. Use gradient clipping at 1.0 for stability. No weight decay is needed on LoRA parameters (the base model already has it). The scaling factor α = r is recommended as a default.

Memory Requirements

Only optimizer states (AdamW: m and v) for LoRA parameters need to be stored, plus the LoRA parameters themselves and gradients. For a 7B model with LoRA (r=8, Wq+Wv): LoRA params = 4.2M, optimizer states = 16.8M, gradients = 4.2M → ~25MB total, vs. 56GB for full fine-tuning. This is roughly a 2,200× reduction in memory for optimizer states.

Limitations and Honest Assessment

⚠️
Limitations to consider:
  • Intrinsic rank is task-dependent: While r = 4-8 works for most tasks, some tasks (especially those requiring significant domain shift) may need higher ranks. The paper does not provide a principled method for choosing r a priori.
  • Layer selection is heuristic: The Wq + Wv recommendation is empirical, not theoretically grounded. For different architectures or training regimes, different choices may be optimal.
  • No theoretical guarantee: The intrinsic low-rank hypothesis is supported empirically but not proven theoretically. There is no guarantee that the task-specific adaptation will always be low-rank.
  • Interaction with other techniques: The paper does not explore LoRA's interaction with other PEFT methods, quantization (addressed by QLoRA in [Paper 07]), or RLHF/DPO training.

Practical Implications and Legacy

LoRA has become arguably the most impactful efficiency technique in modern LLM training and deployment. Its influence extends far beyond the original paper:

  • QLoRA ([Paper 07]): Combined 4-bit quantization with LoRA, enabling fine-tuning of 65B models on a single GPU. The finding that LoRA should be applied to ALL layers (not just attention) when using quantization was a critical extension.
  • Zephyr ([Paper 11]): Used LoRA throughout the dSFT and dDPO pipeline, demonstrating that LoRA is compatible with preference optimization methods.
  • ORPO ([Paper 12]): ORPO's monolithic loss formulation can be applied directly with LoRA, combining SFT and preference optimization in a single stage.
  • DPO ([Paper 10]): While DPO doesn't require LoRA specifically, LoRA is the de facto standard for applying DPO to large models due to memory constraints.

The key insight of LoRA — that fine-tuning updates have low intrinsic rank — has influenced how practitioners think about model adaptation. It suggests that the pre-trained model has already learned most of what it needs to know, and adaptation is about amplifying specific capabilities rather than learning from scratch. This insight is also connected to [Paper 03] (Textbooks Are All You Need), which argues that data quality matters more than scale for fine-tuning, and [Paper 08] (LIMA), which shows that 1,000 high-quality examples suffice for alignment.
